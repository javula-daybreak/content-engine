Everyone reads a turnover chart as a capacity chart. Read the column next to it, the one with case volume in it, and you get a different article.

We took turnover from 34 minutes to 27 over about a year. My environmental services team earned every one of those seven minutes. Total cases per day did not move.

## The number on the dashboard is the one that had a field

Hospitals did not choose to manage turnover time because turnover time is where surgical capacity lives. We manage it because CMS and the surgical societies made first-case starts and room utilization reportable, and because the electronic record can stamp the minute a patient enters a room and the minute the room is clean. Those two timestamps subtract cleanly. Almost nothing else in the perioperative day does.

So the dashboard fills up with the differences between timestamps that exist, and the meeting fills up with the dashboard. **What gets managed is not what matters; it is what subtracted.**

I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this argument that is just a director explaining why the part of the process he owns cannot be measured. That version deserves the eye-roll it gets. I am making a narrower claim: turnover is measured accurately, it improved, and the improvement did not become cases. Both halves of that are on the same report.

## Seven minutes, eleven times a day, given back

Here is where the seven minutes went.

The next patient was not ready, because pre-admission testing had not closed. Anesthesia was in another room, because we were three anesthesiologists short for eight months in 2024 and one short anesthesiologist costs two rooms rather than one. The tray was still in sterile processing. Sometimes the room was genuinely ready and empty and nothing was scheduled into it, which is the one that should keep a COO awake.

None of those is a turnover problem. Every one of them is upstream of the moment the stopwatch starts.

When we actually went looking, 71% of our case delays traced to something that happened before the patient reached the OR. That number is not on any report I receive. There is no field for it. The record will tell you the minute the patient entered the room; it will not tell you why the case that was supposed to be in that room at 9:40 is not.

## Why the obvious fixes do not fix it

The obvious fix is to go faster in the room, and it is obvious because it is the only lever with a number attached.

The second obvious fix is to buy rooms. I wrote that business case. I spent months on it and I withdrew it, because I found a Tuesday in February where six of our rooms sat idle at three in the afternoon while nine cases waited to get on the schedule. We did not have a room shortage. We had eleven block holders, four of them running under 60% utilization, and a block policy that permits reclamation under 70% and has never once been enforced in nine years.

The third obvious fix is a better dashboard, and it is the worst of the three, because it ends in a field somebody has to populate by hand at 6:40 in the morning while a patient waits.

## What is actually broken

The perioperative day is a chain of handoffs between departments that do not report to the same person, and the only segment instrumented end to end is the one that happens inside a room owned by one department.

Pre-admission testing hands a cleared chart to the OR. Sterile processing hands back a tray. Anesthesia hands over a body of people who can be in exactly one room at a time. Scheduling hands over a plan built on assumptions about all three. Every one of those handoffs is a place a case can die, and none of them produces two timestamps you can subtract.

The one time we moved a real number, this is what it was. First-case on-time starts went from 58% to 81% over fourteen months, and the thing that moved it was not the surgeons and it was not the flip. **We moved the pre-admission testing call from three days before surgery to seven.** Four days of extra notice, which is enough time for a send-out lab to come back, for somebody to notice the consent is unsigned, and for a patient with no ride home to arrange one.

Nothing about that is a throughput initiative. It is a phone call, made earlier.

## What changes if you fix it

Four things, and none of them is a project.

The morning huddle stops being a status meeting and becomes three questions: which rooms are staffed, which patients are not cleared, which trays are not back. I inherited a forty-minute huddle and cut it to five minutes; it took four months for people to stop trying to add agenda items to it.

The block committee gets a number it is willing to act on, which is not utilization. It is the count of cases that waited while prime time went unused.

Staffing gets planned against the worst hour instead of the average day, which is the only honest way to plan it; a ratio built on an average is a number somebody wanted to defend in a budget meeting.

And a day-of-surgery cancellation gets priced properly. Ours costs about $4,200 in unrecovered fixed cost. It also costs the patient two weeks and a second round of pre-op labs. Only one of those two numbers appears in any report I get, and it is not the one the patient would pick.

## Why it does not happen anyway

Because every one of those fixes has a name attached to it and the turnover project does not.

Reclaiming block time from four surgeons is a conversation with four surgeons, each of whom can take volume down the road, and the person who has that conversation absorbs the cost of it personally for about eighteen months. Moving the pre-admission testing call four days earlier means a department that does not report to me changing its staffing pattern. Cutting the huddle means telling six directors their agenda item is not a huddle item.

Flipping the room faster means asking the people with the least positional power in the building to hurry.

I withdrew that capital request and I want to be honest about why it existed in the first place. It existed because a capital request is what you write when the real fix requires a conversation you do not want to have. I was not confused about the constraint. I was avoiding it, and I had a spreadsheet that let me avoid it in a way that looked like work.

## The closer

Turnover is the only part of the delay we instrumented, so it became the only part we manage. If your throughput project is a turnover project, you are not measuring the wrong thing; you are measuring the only thing that would hold still long enough to be measured, and then reporting it as though it were the answer.

The four hours you cannot see are still there.
