Everyone reads a turnover chart as a capacity chart. Read the column next to it, the one with case volume in it, and you get a different article.

We took turnover from 34 minutes to 27 over about a year. My environmental services team earned every one of those seven minutes. Total cases per day didn't move.

## The number on the dashboard is the one that had a field

Hospitals didn't start managing turnover time because turnover time is where surgical capacity lives. We manage it because CMS and the surgical societies made first-case starts and room utilization reportable, and because the electronic record can stamp the minute a patient enters a room and the minute the room is clean. Those two timestamps subtract cleanly. Almost nothing else in the perioperative day does.

So the dashboard fills up with the differences between timestamps that exist, and the meeting fills up with the dashboard. **What gets managed isn't what matters; it's what subtracted.**

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this argument that's just a director explaining why the part of the process she owns can't be measured. That version deserves the eye-roll it gets. My claim is narrower: turnover was measured accurately, it improved, and the improvement didn't become cases. Both halves of that are on the same report.

## Seven minutes, eleven times a day, given back

Here's where the seven minutes went.

The next patient wasn't ready, because pre-admission testing hadn't closed. Anesthesia was in another room, because we were three anesthesiologists short for eight months in 2024, and one short anesthesiologist costs you two rooms rather than one. The tray was still in sterile processing. Sometimes the room was genuinely ready and empty and nothing was scheduled into it, which is the one that should keep a COO awake.

None of those is a turnover problem. Every one of them is upstream of the moment the stopwatch starts.

When we went looking, 71% of our case delays traced to something that happened before the patient reached the OR. That number isn't on any report I receive. There's no field for it. The record will tell you the minute the patient entered the room; it won't tell you why the case that was supposed to be in that room at 9:40 isn't.

## Why the obvious fixes don't fix it

The obvious fix is to go faster in the room, and it's obvious because it's the only lever with a number attached to it.

The second obvious fix is to buy rooms. I built that business case last year and I withdrew it, because the scheduling data wouldn't support it: we have eleven block holders, four of them running under 60% utilization, and a block policy that permits reclamation under 70% and hasn't been enforced once in nine years. We weren't short of rooms. We were short of anyone willing to make that phone call.

The third obvious fix is a better dashboard, and it's the worst of the three, because it ends in a field somebody has to populate by hand at 6:40 in the morning while a patient waits.

## What's actually broken

The perioperative day is a chain of handoffs between departments that don't report to the same person, and the only segment instrumented end to end is the one that happens inside a room owned by one department.

Pre-admission testing hands a cleared chart to the OR. Sterile processing hands back a tray. Anesthesia hands over a body of people who can be in exactly one room at a time. Scheduling hands over a plan built on assumptions about all three. Every one of those handoffs is a place a case can die, and not one of them produces two timestamps you can subtract.

The one time we moved a real number, here's what it was. First-case on-time starts went from 58% to 81% over fourteen months, and the thing that moved it wasn't the surgeons and it wasn't the flip. **We moved the pre-admission testing call from three days before surgery to seven.** Four days of extra notice, which is enough time for a send-out lab to come back, for somebody to notice a consent is unsigned, and for a patient with no ride home to arrange one.

Nothing about that is a throughput initiative. It's a phone call, made earlier.

## What changes if you fix it

Four things, and not one of them is a project.

The morning huddle stops being a status meeting and becomes three questions: which rooms are staffed, which patients aren't cleared, which trays aren't back. I inherited a forty-minute huddle and cut it to five; it took four months for people to stop trying to add agenda items to it.

The block committee gets a number it's willing to act on, and it isn't utilization. It's the count of cases that waited while prime time went unused.

Staffing gets planned against the worst hour instead of the average day, which is the only honest way to plan it. A ratio built on an average is a number somebody wanted to defend in a budget meeting.

And a day-of-surgery cancellation gets priced properly. Ours runs about $4,200 in unrecovered fixed cost. It also costs the patient two weeks and a second round of pre-op labs. Only one of those two numbers shows up in any report I get, and it isn't the one the patient would pick.

## Why it doesn't happen anyway

Because every one of those fixes has a name attached to it, and the turnover project doesn't.

Reclaiming block time means a conversation with four surgeons, each of whom can take volume down the road, and whoever has that conversation absorbs the cost of it personally for about eighteen months. Moving the pre-admission testing call four days earlier means a department that doesn't report to me changing its staffing pattern. Cutting the huddle means telling six directors their agenda item isn't a huddle item.

Flipping the room faster means asking the people with the least positional power in the building to hurry.

I'll give you the version of this I'm least proud of. In 2024 I built a schedule that assumed we'd fill two of three open anesthesia positions by March. We filled none. I'd already committed that block time. In April I cancelled four cases in a single day, and I let the scheduler tell the first two surgeons by phone. I should have made those two calls myself. The third one I told in person, and he's the only one of the four who still trusts my scheduling.

The cost of an operations decision lands on somebody. Usually it isn't the person who made it.

## The closer

Turnover is the only part of the delay we instrumented, so it became the only part we manage. That isn't a measurement error. It's a measurement boundary, and it falls exactly where the departments do.

The four hours nobody stamps are still there.
